The second race to the Moon is already on.

This time the prize isn't a flag. It's a place to stay. And the best places to stay may be underground.

1969 – 1972

Twelve people walked on the Moon. Then everyone went home.

Six Apollo landings added up to about 80 hours of moonwalking, all on the near side and all within a few dozen degrees of the equator. The last crew left in December 1972.

For the next half-century, nobody went back. The first race was won by arriving. Nobody had a reason to stay.

Now there are two serious programs, and both are moving.

The new objective

Apollo was a visit. This race is about who stays.

China has committed to landing its first astronauts on the Moon before 2030. That deadline is why this is a race again and not a schedule.

NASA's Moon Base plan uses robotic missions through 2029 to pick infrastructure sites before astronauts arrive, then moves into power, cargo and communications. China describes the ILRS as a long-duration base with its own transport, energy, navigation and resource use.

Both are heading for the south pole, where permanently shadowed craters may hold water ice.

Once the Moon becomes infrastructure, knowing the terrain pays off on every mission after.

The problem with the surface

Everything built on the surface has to fight the Moon.

Near the equator the ground swings from about +120 °C at midday to about −170 °C at night, over a day that lasts 29.5 Earth days. With no air and no magnetic field, radiation and micrometeoroids arrive unfiltered. Every kilogram of shielding a surface base needs has to be launched or dug.

In the shaded floor of the Mare Tranquillitatis pit, models fit to orbiter measurements put the temperature near +17 °C, day and night.

A second geography

The Moon has holes in it. Some of them lead somewhere.

Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter imagery has turned up more than 200 pits. A few, in the dark lava plains, look like collapsed skylights into lava tubes. In 2024 a team re-analyzing 2010 radar data found a cave conduit running sideways from the Mare Tranquillitatis pit.

Meters of rock overhead are free radiation shielding. A stable interior is free thermal control. The volume is already there.

An orbiter can tell you a hole exists. It can't tell you whether a habitat fits inside.

SLAM · How it works

SLAM goes inside and measures the space.

A surface station sets down near the rim. It carries the solar power, the radio link to Earth, the data archive and a fixed reference point for the whole survey.

Descent

A compact autonomous rover is lowered over the edge on one tether that carries power, fiber-optic data and the load.

Tether payout and tension double as sensors: how far down the rover is, and whether the line has snagged.

Simultaneous localization and mapping

At the bottom, it drives into the dark.

3D LiDAR, cameras with their own lighting, an IMU and wheel odometry feed SLAM: the rover works out where it is from the shape of what it sees, with no GPS and no line of sight to Earth. People set the objective and the safety limits. The rover handles the next meter.

Failure-tolerant data

Every scan streams up the tether the moment it's taken.

If the rover stops, everything it already mapped is safe at the surface. A survey that ends early still returns a usable map.

The product

Google Maps and Street View for the lunar underground.

What comes back is a registered 3D survey, not a breadcrumb trail. An engineer can measure a clearance, look at the actual wall at that coordinate, compare routes and estimate usable volume before any hardware leaves Earth.

Select
Compare candidate caves on volume, access, slope, hazards and uncertainty. Reject a bad site before it becomes a mission.
Design
Fit habitats, power, comms and emergency egress against measured geometry instead of an assumed tube.
Operate
Use the same spatial layer for navigation, inspection and construction underground.

Why now

Getting to the Moon is becoming something you can buy.

17CLPS lunar deliveries awarded by NASA
60+payloads assigned across them
$2.6BCLPS contract ceiling through Nov. 2028

NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services buys end-to-end delivery: integration, launch, landing and operations. In 2026 Astrobotic won two Moon Base missions for 2028, and Intuitive Machines took a $180.4M award for a larger cargo-class delivery.

SLAM doesn't need to build a rocket or a lander. It builds the payload and buys the ride.

Build log · October 2026

The lunar rover lives in software first.

SLAM's digital twin runs in MuJoCo with seven generations of rover and tether geometry, exact-CAD interference audits and synthetic LiDAR. The Earth rover is a deliberately cheap surrogate for the mapping and tether system, built to prove the loop in caves and mines before any flight hardware exists.

Roadmap

Prove it cheaply on Earth. Then go.

Next

  1. Earth surrogateZero-light perception, SLAM accuracy, full lowering and tether payout in real caves and tunnels.
  2. Digital lunar vehicleKeep the flight rover in simulation while requirements mature: terrain, autonomy, structure, thermal, mass.
  3. Flight developmentVacuum, thermal and dust qualification once the mapping thesis has evidence.
  4. Fund and flyNASA SBIR/STTR, lunar programs and strategic partners, then a CLPS-class ride.

Then

Qualify candidate caves → engineering surveys for habitation → underground operations → a searchable Moon-wide atlas → Mars, where the USGS has catalogued more than a thousand candidate cave entrances.

Before anyone builds underground, someone has to map underground.

SLAM intends to own that map.

Map the Moon. Build from the map. Take the platform farther.
