SLAMSubsurface Lunar Atlas Mission

The race back to the Moon is on again.

Fifty years after Apollo, the United States and China are both going back, and this time they plan to stay. Some of the best places to stay may be underground.

Scroll

1969 – 1972

Twelve people walked on the Moon. Then everyone went home.

Six Apollo landings added up to about 80 hours of moonwalking, all on the near side and all close to the equator. The last crew left in December 1972.

Nobody went back for over fifty years. The first race was won by arriving. Nobody had a reason to stay.

2019 – 2035

Now there are two serious programs, and both are moving.

China · CNSA / CMSA

  1. 2019Chang'e-4 makes the first soft landing on the far side.
  2. 2020Chang'e-5 returns the first lunar samples since 1976.
  3. 2024Chang'e-6 returns the first samples ever taken from the far side.
  4. 2026Chang'e-7: orbiter, lander, rover and hopper aimed at south-pole water ice.
  5. <2030First Chinese astronauts on the Moon: an official commitment to land a crew before 2030, using Mengzhou, the Lanyue lander and Long March 10.
  6. 2035International Lunar Research Station basic model (stated target).

United States · NASA + industry

  1. 2022Artemis I sends an uncrewed Orion around the Moon and back.
  2. 2024IM-1 Odysseus: first US landing since 1972, flown by a company.
  3. 2025Blue Ghost 1 lands and runs a full commercial surface mission.
  4. Apr 2026Artemis II flies four astronauts around the far side, the first crew near the Moon in 54 years.
  5. →2029Moon Base Phase One: robots scout infrastructure sites before crews arrive.

The new objective

Apollo was a visit. This race is about who stays.

China has committed to landing its first astronauts on the Moon before 2030. That deadline is why this is a race again and not a schedule.

NASA's Moon Base plan sends robots through 2029 to pick infrastructure sites before astronauts arrive. China describes the ILRS as a long-duration base with its own power, transport and resource use. Both are aiming for the south pole, where permanently shadowed craters may hold water ice.

Staying means living somewhere. The question is where.

Why go underground · 1 of 4

The surface is hostile. A lava tube is a ready-made shelter.

The Moon has no atmosphere and no global magnetic field. Galactic cosmic rays and solar particle storms hit the ground unfiltered, and a crew in a surface habitat absorbs all of it.

Several meters of rock overhead stop most of that dose before it reaches anyone. A lava tube comes with the rock already in place.

Why go underground · 2 of 4

No 300-degree temperature swings.

A lunar day lasts 29.5 Earth days. Near the equator the surface climbs to about +120 °C in the afternoon and drops to about −170 °C through two weeks of night. Every surface system has to survive that cycle again and again.

In the shaded floor of the Mare Tranquillitatis pit, models fit to orbiter data put the temperature near +17 °C, day and night. Steady temperatures mean smaller heaters, smaller radiators and less power spent just staying alive.

Why go underground · 3 of 4

Out of the line of fire.

Micrometeoroids hit the surface constantly and fast. Every landing and every impact throws abrasive dust that wears out seals, joints, optics and radiators.

Underground, the roof takes the impacts and very little dust settles. Equipment lasts longer and needs less maintenance.

Why go underground · 4 of 4

The volume is already there.

Protecting a surface base means launching shielding from Earth or having robots pile regolith over it. Both cost mass, power and time.

Inside a lava tube, nature has already built the shield and the room. Every kilogram not spent on shielding can go to life support, science or more crew.

A protected void is only valuable once you know its shape, how to get in and what's inside.

They exist

The Moon has holes in it. Some of them lead somewhere.

Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter imagery has turned up more than 200 pits. A few, in the dark lava plains, look like collapsed skylights into lava tubes.

In 2024 a team re-analyzing radar data from 2010 found a cave conduit running sideways from the Mare Tranquillitatis pit. It was the first direct evidence of an accessible cave below the lunar surface.

The problem

Nobody has measured one from the inside.

From orbit you see a hole, a shadow and a sunlit sliver of wall. You can't see how far the cave goes, how high the ceiling is, whether the floor is drivable or whether a habitat would fit.

Without those answers, no agency can commit a mission, and no company can design a habitat for a cave.

A cave doesn't become infrastructure until it's measured.

The solution · SLAM

Lower a mapping robot into the cave on a tether.

A surface station sets down near the rim. It carries solar power, the radio link to Earth, the data archive, the tether reel and a fixed reference point for the whole survey.

Descent

A compact autonomous rover is lowered over the edge on one tether that carries power, fiber-optic data and the load.

The reel measures payout and tension, so the station always knows how far down the rover is and whether the line has snagged.

Simultaneous localization and mapping

At the bottom, it drives into the dark.

3D LiDAR, cameras with their own lighting, an IMU and wheel odometry feed SLAM: the rover works out where it is from the shape of what it sees, with no GPS and no line of sight to Earth. People set the objective and the safety limits. The rover handles the next meter.

Failure-tolerant data

Every scan streams up the tether the moment it's taken.

If the rover stops, everything it already mapped is safe at the surface. A survey that ends early still returns a usable map.

The device

A small rover built for one job.

Small enough to lower through a skylight, low and wide enough to drive a rubble floor in total darkness. Everything it needs to map is on board, and everything it learns goes up the cable.

Sensors

It sees without daylight.

  • 3D LiDAR measures the cave as millions of points with its own laser, so it works in total darkness.
  • Stereo cameras with onboard lighting record what each wall actually looks like.
  • IMU and wheel odometry track motion between scans.
  • Onboard compute runs SLAM and the autonomy loop: sense, localize, map, choose, move.

Tether

One cable does three jobs.

  • Copper conductors carry power down from the station's solar array, so the rover doesn't need a large battery.
  • Optical fiber carries every scan up to the surface as it's taken.
  • A tensile braid takes the load for lowering, holding and recovering the rover.

The product

Google Maps and Street View for the lunar underground.

Every scan is registered into one 3D model of the cave: geometry, matching imagery, the rover's path, where the floor is drivable, a fixed reference frame and a confidence value on every measurement.

That map is the product. Here is what people do with it.

Use 1 · Select

Pick the right cave before anything flies.

Mission planners compare candidate caves on ceiling height, narrowest clearance, floor slope, rockfall hazards and usable volume, each with an uncertainty attached.

A bad site gets rejected on Earth instead of after landing.

Use 2 · Design

Build the habitat to fit the real cave.

Habitat and infrastructure teams fit modules against measured walls instead of an assumed tube. They route power and comms from the skylight, place anchors and lighting, and plan an emergency exit before any hardware leaves Earth.

Use 3 · Operate

Then run the base from the same map.

Rovers and crews navigate with it. Repeat scans catch rockfall and change over time. Science teams use it to aim ground-penetrating radar, spectrometers and radiation sensors, and to find ice or other resources.

The value is the decision the map lets someone make before hardware leaves Earth.

Before anyone builds underground, someone has to map underground.

SLAM intends to own that map.

Map the Moon. Build from the map.
